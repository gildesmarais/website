## A warning beforehand

This post does not contain any photos, but it might be disturbing to read anyway.
This post is not intended as an instruction manual. You will need experience to do this right and the best/legal way possible.  
If you're interested in deepening your knowledge, ask a slaughter, a butcher or your local administration for pointers.

## The killing procedure

The killing procedure, in Germany/Austria, is like this:

- isolate a pig from the herd
- daze it (e.g. with bolt gun)
- cut the throat
- let it bleed out completely[^1]

A pig like this weighs around 80 to 90kg.

The pig will still twitch as its nerves are still active.

The killing procedure differs in other regions in the world for many reasons (laws, religion, tradition, personal preferences, etc).
The laws might not always permit the most pain- and stress-less method. For example, some do not daze the animal and just cut the throat with a cleanly lead special knife.[^2]

Many traditions demand to have a toast on the now dead pig. So did we.

## From a dead pig to two halves

- place the dead pig in boiling water
- remove fur thoroughly by scraping it off (with a [<q>Kratzglocke</q>](https://duckduckgo.com/?q=kratzglocke&iax=images&ia=images#))
- burn the remaining bristles
- hang the pig with its hind legs up
- cut it open: from the anus, via belly to the head while removing innards[^3]
- saw it open: from the anus, via the back, through the head

Now you have two halves. You hang the halves for a day (or longer) to dry out and easen the dead meat up.

## Butchering: cutting halves to pieces

Depending on your butcher tradition, you start cutting pieces out of the halves.

There are a bunch of different cutting methods, depending on the cuisine and the pig's race mostly.
Read more about in the Wikipedia[^4] or your favorite non-veggie cookbook[^5].

As the race is known for good (and lots of) fats, we concentrated on belly bacon, etc.
The end result are pieces of meat you can buy at your butcher's outlet: rips, cutlet, pork chops, neck, roasts, bacon, ...

## Observations

### Avoiding stress

When choosing a pig to kill, you want to avoid any stress possible. The situation when entering the sty should be totally normal for the pigs.[^6]

Let's change the perspective from pig to human for a second and imagine the situation yourself: if you know your life will be taken by that person entering the sty, you'd be pumped with adrenaline and fight back. Wouldn't it be nicer if it would just get dark in front of your eyes and from that point on you'd notice nothing anymore?

Switching the perspective back to the pig: the adrenaline will cause blood pressure to raise and thus fill all organs with water and oxygen. Blood in meat is to be avoided (it spoils) and water lowers the meat quality.

I'm afraid most pigs who will have this experience won't have such <q>good</q> conditions. I can't see a pig being carried in a cramped lorry to the slaughter house for hours being relaxed and calm.

### Coping with routine

Our workshop leader, who executed the killing procedure, killed plenty of animals in his life. Still, while the procedure was executed professionally, it was not an easy thing to do for him. It touched him.  
This impressed me and I wondered how people working in an industrial scale slaughterhouse cope with it.

### Similarities to the human body

The pig's body and its organs are similar to a human. I've analyzed a pig's heart in the Biology course in school. I opened one with a scalpel and comprehended how the two chambers work. That was decades ago and thus the connection was lost. The organs of a pig mostly exist and work the same in a human.

### A well feed pig tastes amazing

It's true. There is nothing more to add to the headline.

### Lard makes a smooth skin

While I usually struggle in winter to maintain a smooth skin on my hands, that day was different. My skin was very soft.

Would I recommend lard as skin care?   
Well, it has its pros, but the con is a distinct smell. My verdict: no.

Applying animal produced substances on skin is not that uncommon as it sounds. [Lanolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanolin) is widely used in skin care products and is sheep secrete.

### The change from animal to product

I still struggle to determine when my mind switched from calling the object _animal_ to _product_. I think that change happened closely after the throat was cut and blood was flowing. As soon as that switch happened, I felt personally obligated to make the best use of the product. That means to use everything the pig gave us.

### Using everything from the pig

Except some innards (lung, heart, stomach), we used basically everything.

- we caught the blood to make blood sausage (heat it up and it will curdle).
- we cooked both halves of the head in water, to easily scrape the meat and to use in [Liverwurst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverwurst).
- we warmed the cut-off fat slowly and produced lard which we conserved in jars.
- we processed small pieces of meat, which are unusable as a whole, to make [Bratwurst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bratwurst).
- we used the cleaned intestine for the Bratwurst.

With the innards we prepared small dishes for instant consumption:

- scrambled eggs with brain and chives
- (scraped of the contents of the) splenic and fried them to eat on bread
- grilled liver with onions
- sliced kidney

### Lost connection meat<>animal, and decline of knowledge across generations

Later that week I talked to my dad about the day. For him, it was a normal thing to do in winter but he hasn't done it for decades. Back in the days he helped the family or neighbors to slaughter a pig. And it seems pretty normal for the generation who lived in 1960 / 1970.

That made me realize that the knowledge about slaughtering and its practical appliance slowly gets lost. It's not widespread available anymore. It's concentrated on a few people.  

For me that knowledge is mandatory. It keeps a connection from the meat at the butchers outlet to the pig. I realizied the home slaughtering was a missing link.

### People are skeptic on homemade Liverwurst

I brought a jar of conserved Liverwurst back home and brought it to the office the other day. I told some colleagues about my experience. Some where eager to try it. But the majority wasn't so keen.  
They usually do not retain from trying the food I bring and offer them to try. Somehow people where doubtful about the Liverwurst. My partner made the exact same experience with her colleagues.

And I wonder why that is?  
Do people trust the meat industry more? Were they offended when I told them which parts of the pig were in it? Do they think the industrially produced liverwurst is of better quality? Or mine unsafe to consume?

Maybe we should have ground the meats finer so it would have been a unicolored mass without tiny chunks of meat in it. Or maybe we should have use pickling salt to keep a shiny red instead of gray product. ;)

I don't mind too much about their reaction, don't get me wrong. I suspect people just don't want to get confronted with the whole process when eating meat.

### Changing habits

Did I change my eating habits because of this experience?  
Yes, and I remain a carnivore. I buy only _biological_ raised meat when possible and avoid low-cost mass product meat.

I still eat sausages although I know what's in it.

## Closing

I'd recommend anyone, if you ever have the chance to witness a home slaughtering of an animal, to take part in it.

It won't be a joyful day, but a day full of physical work, in your life, but a day you will remember and it might change your opinion on some things (in whatever way).
